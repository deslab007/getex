Conducting VENM Assessments: What They Involve and How Getex Can Help

27 October 2026 · Justin Thompson-Laing · 7 min read

Why VENM matters

A VENM assessment confirms that excavated soil or rock is natural, uncontaminated and fit to move off site or be reused without being treated as a contaminated waste. Getting that call right is often the difference between sending material to a receiving site at low cost and paying landfill rates for General Solid Waste or worse.

Virgin Excavated Natural Material (VENM) is the cleanest classification available for excavated material in NSW. It covers natural clay, gravel, sand, soil and rock fines dug or quarried from land that has not been contaminated by industrial, commercial, mining or agricultural activity, and that contains no sulfidic ores or soils and no other waste.

Because VENM can be accepted by quarries, fill sites and development projects that will not take anything else, a defensible VENM report is a commercial document as much as a technical one. Receiving sites rely on it, and so do regulators if the material is ever questioned.

The regulatory framework

VENM is defined in Schedule 1 of the Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 and applied through the NSW EPA Waste Classification Guidelines Part 1: Classifying Waste (2014). That definition is the test every Getex VENM report is written against.

Material that is natural but does not meet the VENM definition may still be reusable as Excavated Natural Material (ENM). ENM is governed by a resource recovery order, The Excavated Natural Material Order 2014, made under the POEO (Waste) Regulation 2014. The EPA still lists that order, dated 21 November 2014, as current (NSW EPA).

Material that meets neither test is classified as waste under the Guidelines and goes to a licensed facility.

Getex reports also draw on supporting references: NEPM 2013 Schedule B(2) for sample handling, the NSW eSPADE soil landscape service, the EPA contaminated land register, the acid sulfate soil risk maps and, where needed, Site Investigations for Urban Salinity (DLWC, 2002).

You need one whenever excavated material is leaving a site for reuse, or arriving at one, and the receiving party wants proof it is clean natural material. In Getex’s project files the requests fall into a handful of recurring situations.

How Getex conducts a VENM assessment

Every Getex VENM assessment combines a desktop check, a site inspection, sampling and laboratory analysis, then tests the results against a fixed set of criteria. The material is classified VENM only if it passes every one.

  1. Desktop review. We check the EPA contaminated land register and the acid sulfate soil risk maps, and pull the expected soil landscape and bedrock from eSPADE. We also ask the client for site history.
  2. Services clearance. Sampling locations are cleared through Before You Dig plans and a services locator before any ground is broken.
  3. Site inspection. A consultant inspects the material and the surrounding area for signs of contamination: surface staining, stored chemicals, above or below ground tanks, and neighbouring activities.
  4. Sampling. In-situ material is sampled from test pits on a systematic grid, with a separate sample from each layer where fill sits over natural ground. Stockpiles are sampled around their faces, scaled to volume.
  5. Laboratory analysis. Samples go to the laboratory on a standard 5-day turnaround. A 24-hour turnaround is available, typically at about double the analysis cost.
  6. Assessment and reporting. Results are compared against the criteria below and the classification is stated in the report.

Sampling density depends on the material. As a guide from recent jobs, a 15 m³ stockpile took 3 samples, a 630 m³ stockpile took 10, and a 550 m² residential site took 5 grid locations under the ENM Order.

What you receive

You receive a VENM Assessment Report that states a clear classification and the evidence behind it, reviewed by a Certified Environmental Practitioner (Site Contamination). Receiving sites can check every step.

The report contains:

  • Scope, site address, sampling date and report number
  • Material description: type, location and estimated volume
  • Number of samples, analytes tested and sample handling method
  • The assessment criteria applied
  • A results table for each material, ending in a stated classification
  • Limitations of the assessment
  • Appendices: site map, photographs, sample results against criteria, and the laboratory reports

Where the material does not make VENM, the work is not wasted. Getex scopes jobs so the same sampling feeds the next classification down:

  1. VENM if every criterion is met, with a VENM certificate.
  2. ENM if the material is natural but fails the VENM test and meets the ENM Order, with an ENM certificate.
  3. Waste classification if it meets neither, so the material can go to the right licensed facility.

A classification reflects the material at the sampled locations on the day of sampling. If a stockpile is later added to or mixed, the report no longer covers it.

Common issues that cost time or money

Most delays and failed classifications on VENM jobs are avoidable if they are planned for before excavation starts.

  • Fill mixed into natural material. Where fill or topsoil sits over natural ground, digging it out together can turn reusable VENM into waste. Getex samples each layer separately so they can be excavated and classified separately.
  • Unknown volumes. Sample numbers depend on area and volume. Where these are not yet known, as on staged infrastructure trenching, a unit rate per location keeps the job moving.
  • Little site history. A VENM call rests partly on what the land has been used for. The more history a client can provide, the stronger the conclusion.
  • Receiving site requirements. Some receiving sites want more than a VENM classification, such as a salinity class. Confirming their acceptance criteria first avoids a second round of sampling.
  • Services not cleared. Sampling cannot start until underground services are cleared. Supplying Before You Dig plans and a locator report up front saves a delay.
  • Laboratory turnaround. The standard turnaround is 5 days. Rush analysis is available but roughly doubles analysis cost, so sampling early in the programme is cheaper.
  • Changes after sampling. Material added to a stockpile after sampling is not covered by the report. Keep classified stockpiles separate and protected.
