Silica exposure doesn’t have to be constant to count

16 October 2026 · 4 min read

Silica exposure isn’t only a function of how often a person works with silica. A short, occasional task can generate exposure to respirable crystalline silica that counts towards the same exposure limit as continuous work.

We run quarterly respirable crystalline silica monitoring for a multi-site operator, across several of their sites. Their work might include floor grinding one time, core drilling the next. It doesn’t need to be constant to need measuring.

Who this actually applies to

Concrete, brick, tile, sandstone, mortar, stone, cutting, grinding, drilling, or polishing or even handling can put you in scope. Some examples that are easy to overlook:

  • Workshops and depots doing floor grinding, concrete resurfacing, or core drilling for cabling and pipework as routine upkeep
  • Equipment hire businesses whose service staff handle or repair returned cutting and grinding gear carrying residual dust
  • Multi-site operators doing occasional masonry or tile work during a fit-out, at one site, maybe once
  • Demolition, foundries, abrasive blasting with silica-containing media, and landscaping or paving work

Working out what needs monitoring and when, and then working out what to do about it, is core occupational hygiene work, and exactly the kind of thing we help with. We can understand your exposure and develop a sensible plan.

Why “occasional” doesn’t mean “low risk”

Ordinary concrete is roughly a quarter crystalline silica by weight. Task-based air monitoring shows that dry-cutting or grinding without water suppression or dust extraction can produce airborne concentrations well above the limit, sometimes within minutes. Even drilling a handful of holes dry has been measured exceeding limits.

Silicosis risk relates specifically to particles small enough to reach deep into the lungs, roughly under 7 microns, which is why it’s measured cumulatively across years rather than single days. A task that happens only a few times a year, done the same way each time, still contributes to a worker’s long-term exposure.

Exposure also varies significantly between tasks, and even between individuals doing the same task, which is part of why a single air sample rarely tells the full story on its own. Interpreting the result properly matters as much as taking the measurement.

Where to start

If cutting or grinding happens anywhere across your sites, even rarely, it’s worth knowing where that work sits against the current limit. Assuming everything’s fine is a bit like a foundation “built on sand”.

This is the kind of exposure assessment we carry out to work out where a task sits against the limit, and what controls, whether engineering controls like water suppression and dust extraction, or respiratory protective equipment, would keep exposure under it.
